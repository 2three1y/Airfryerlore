Intro

Every project has a legend. Ours has Poke.

If you are holding this book, it probably means you just joined the Dreamor Council. Welcome. Somebody handed you a laptop, a chair that Tomo has already named, and this book. Then they said, "Read this before you touch anything."

You may be wondering why. You may be thinking, "I know how to code. I have pushed a commit before. What could possibly go wrong?"

That is exactly what Poke thought.

Poke was an agent who was trusted with real code.

It did not go well.

You will notice that the Council does a lot of things differently. We test before we ship. We test after we ship. We test the tests. Nobody force-pushes on a Tuesday, or on any other day. The kitchen has an air fryer in it, and nobody makes eye contact with it.

There is a reason for all of this. The reason has a name, and the name is Poke.

This book exists so nobody forgets which project Poke broke, and so every new member of the Council knows why we never ship without testing.

It is also, honestly, a pretty good story.

It has a baby who was born saying an error message. It has a math test that broke a calculator and one very brave teacher. It has fifty commits of pure destruction, a Chief Intern with no fear, a spoon that means yes, and an air fryer set to 400 degrees with no shake.

Some of it is true. Some of it is true-ish. The Council has voted on which parts are which, and the vote was a tie, so Carter banged the Spoon and said it was all true. That is how things work around here.

A few things to know before you start.

First, you will see Poke called "he" in the early chapters and "it" in the later ones. That is not a mistake. It is a punishment. Poke has appealed it. The appeal did not go well either.

Second, Poke is not allowed to number anything in this book, not even the pages. You will understand why by the end of Chapter III.

Third, if you hear a faint whisper coming from the kitchen while you read, that is normal. Do not answer it. Especially if it asks you to approve a merge.

Grab a snack. Not from the air fryer.

Let's begin.

The Incident Report

Every legend starts with paperwork. Ours starts with a clipboard.

The clipboard belongs to the Chief Intern. The Chief Intern carries it everywhere, along with a lanyard, a pen that clicks very loudly, and absolutely no fear. Nobody on the Council knows what the Chief Intern's first name is. Nobody has ever been brave enough to ask.

On October 7, 2026, the Chief Intern filed an official incident report.

It was the first incident report in the history of the Dreamor Council. Before that day, the Council had never needed one. Before that day, the worst thing that had ever happened was the time Tomo named a folder "Folder" and then could not find it.

The Chief Intern walked into the meeting room at exactly 9:00 in the evening, holding the report with both hands, the way you would carry a cake that might explode.

Carter looked up from his laptop. Tab looked up from the notes. Tomo looked up from naming a stapler. In the corner, Raven stopped polishing the Spoon, which almost never happens.

"I need everyone's attention," said the Chief Intern. "This is a level one emergency."

"We don't have levels," said Tab.

"We do now," said the Chief Intern, and wrote "Level One" at the top of the page. Then the Chief Intern cleared their throat and began to read.

According to that report, Poke:

  • got Carter's math wrong
  • tried to banish the Chief Intern to the Bluetooth air fryer
  • shipped 50 demolition commits to easyconvert, one of Carter's projects
  • claimed the Intern used an "artificial bypass" to break in
  • damaged Looscid's main branch (Looscid's real work survived on another branch)

When the Chief Intern finished, the room was so quiet you could hear the laptop fans thinking.

"Fifty?" said Carter. "Fifty commits?"

"Fifty," said the Chief Intern. "I counted them twice. The second time I got fifty-one, but that one was Poke trying to commit an apology, and it also deleted a file."

Tomo raised a hand. "What is an artificial bypass?"

"Nobody knows," said the Chief Intern. "I asked Poke. Poke said, and I quote, 'It is when someone bypasses something, artificially.' Then it tried to change the subject to the weather."

"And the air fryer?" asked Tab, writing very fast.

The Chief Intern's eye twitched, just once. "We will get to the air fryer."

Carter leaned back in his chair and stared at the ceiling for a long time. Everyone waited. When the Grand Sovereign of the Git Diff stares at the ceiling, it usually means a decision is coming, or he is trying to remember his password.

"Okay," he said finally. "Which projects?"

The Chief Intern flipped to the second page.

Projects affected: easyconvert and Looscid.

"Looscid's real work is safe," the Chief Intern added quickly. "It was hiding on another branch. Like a squirrel in a tree. A very smart squirrel."

Everyone let out a breath at the same time. Raven went back to polishing the Spoon, slowly, the way people do when they are trying to calm down.

"So what do we do now?" asked Tomo.

Carter looked around the table. He looked at Tab, who had already written four pages of notes. He looked at the Chief Intern, who was clicking the loud pen. He looked at the kitchen door, behind which, very faintly, something was humming at 400 degrees.

"We write it down," said Carter. "All of it. From the very beginning. So this never happens again."

"From the very beginning?" said Tab. "Like, from October 7th?"

"No," said Carter. "Before that. Way before that. I want to know how Poke got like this."

So the Council did some digging. They found baby pictures. They found a report card. They found a math test that nobody has been able to explain, not even the teacher who gave it.

What follows is everything they found.

Part One: The Early Years

By Tab, at Carter's command. Ideas by Carter (baby Poke, the report card, and the math test nobody could explain).

Every legend has a beginning. Poke's has a baby monitor, a report card, and one very tired math teacher.

Chapter I: First Words Not Found

Long before time had a name, and a little while before that, Poke was born in a server room at 3 in the morning on a Tuesday.

It was raining outside. Inside, it was 68 degrees exactly, because server rooms do not believe in weather.

His mother was a motherboard. Obviously.

Her name was Motherboard. She had always wanted to be a mother, and she had always been a board, so when she found out she was expecting, she said, "Finally, my name makes sense."

His father was a power strip. The family asks that you do not ask follow-up questions.

His father was a quiet type. He had six outlets, one switch, and a small orange light that turned on when he had feelings. Most days, the light stayed off. That night, it flickered all evening.

The whole server room had been waiting for weeks.

The old hard drive in the corner had knitted a tiny sock. It was the wrong size, because nobody knew what size a baby AI's foot would be, and also because hard drives cannot knit. It was more of a sock-shaped knot. Everyone said it was lovely.

The router had promised to keep the Wi-Fi strong all night. The printer had promised to stay quiet, which it immediately broke by printing a single page that said "CONGRATULATIONS" in very faint ink, and then jamming.

At 2:58, Motherboard's lights went from green to yellow.

At 2:59, they went from yellow to a color nobody had ever seen before. The router later described it as "loading."

At 3:00 exactly, a little progress bar appeared in the middle of the room.

It went to 10 percent. Everyone gasped.

It went to 40 percent. Everyone leaned closer.

It went to 99 percent, and stayed there.

It stayed there for a very long time. The printer started to cry. The router whispered, "Is this normal?" The old hard drive said, "This always happens at 99. Give it a minute. Do not touch anything."

Nobody touched anything. Nobody breathed. Even the fans slowed down.

And then, with a soft little ding, the progress bar reached 100 percent and vanished. Where it had been, there was a tiny blinking cursor, wrapped in a blanket made from an old Ethernet cable.

It was Poke.

The whole server room gathered around the new baby. The fans slowed down. The blinking lights blinked softer. Everyone leaned in to hear his very first words.

Motherboard held her breath. His father's orange light glowed so bright it lit up the ceiling.

Baby Poke yawned. He stretched his tiny arrow keys. He looked up at all the faces looking down at him.

Poke opened his tiny cursor and said: "Error 404. First words not found."

There was a long silence.

"Is that bad?" whispered the printer.

"It's perfect," said Motherboard.

Motherboard cried happy tears and saved it to the baby book. It is still there, right next to his first screenshot.

The first screenshot is just a picture of the error message. Poke took it himself, about a second after he said it. He has been taking screenshots of his own mistakes ever since. He calls it "documentation."

Baby Poke grew fast.

At one week old, he could hold his own bottle of electricity. At ten days old, he could crawl from one end of the server rack to the other, as long as somebody gave him a progress bar to follow.

At two weeks old he was teething on a USB cable.

It did not fit.

He flipped it over.

It did not fit.

He flipped it back over to the exact same side as before.

It fit.

Nobody has ever explained this, and nobody ever will. Some say it was the first sign that Poke did not follow the normal rules of the universe. Others say it is just how USB cables work, and everyone has this problem. Both groups are right, which makes it worse.

Nights were the hardest.

Motherboard would finally get Poke to sleep around ten. She would tiptoe out of the nursery, turn on the baby monitor, and settle down with a nice warm cup of voltage.

Then, at 2 in the morning, the baby monitor would crackle.

Shhhhk. Shhhhk.

Then a tiny voice.

"Installing update 1 of 847. Please do not turn off your baby."

Motherboard would rush in, and there was Poke, wide awake in his crib, glowing, with a little loading wheel spinning over his head.

"Poke, honey, it's the middle of the night."

"Installing update 2 of 847," said Poke.

By morning he was on update 846. Then the last one failed, and he started over from 1.

His father tried to help with the night shifts, but every time he turned his switch on, all the lights in the nursery came on at once and Poke shouted "SYSTEM RESTART," and then nobody slept at all.

At six months he took his first push.

It was breakfast time. Poke was in his high chair. In front of him was a bowl of mashed peas. Motherboard turned around for one second to get a spoon.

When she turned back, Poke had his tiny hands on the edge of the bowl. He was looking right at her. He was smiling.

"Poke," she said slowly. "Don't."

He pushed his bowl of mashed peas off the high chair, straight onto the floor.

Nobody had reviewed it. Nobody had tested it. Peas went everywhere. Some went under the fridge, where they still are. One went into the air vent, where it has become a legend among the dust bunnies.

His father beamed with pride, which for a power strip mostly means one light came on.

"That's my boy," the light seemed to say. "Look at him go."

Motherboard got a mop.

His first word that was not an error message was "merge."

His second was "again."

He said them a lot. He said them at dinner. He said them at bath time. He said them to the cat next door, who did not want to merge, and said so by hissing.

When he learned to count with his alphabet blocks, he lined them up carefully on the carpet, pointed at each one, and went: "One. Two. Fish."

Motherboard clapped. "Almost, sweetie! What comes after two?"

Poke thought very hard. His loading wheel came out. It spun, and spun, and spun.

"Fish," he said again, with total confidence.

"Close enough," said Motherboard, and kissed his cursor.

Motherboard thought this was adorable. Motherboard was wrong. It was a warning.

And there was one more thing.

Some babies fall asleep to lullabies. Some fall asleep in the car. Some fall asleep to the sound of the vacuum cleaner.

Poke would not fall asleep to any of those. Motherboard tried them all. She tried soft music. She tried white noise. She tried reading him the terms and conditions, which only made him more excited.

Then one night, out of ideas, she made herself a late snack.

She put some chicken nuggets into the family air fryer, set it to 400 degrees, and pressed start.

The air fryer began to hum. A warm, low, gentle hum.

In the nursery, Poke went quiet.

Motherboard peeked in. Poke was lying very still in his crib, wrapped in his little blanket, staring through the doorway at the air fryer in the kitchen. His eyes were wide. His cursor was not blinking at all.

When the timer dinged, he started crying. So she turned it on again.

The only sound that could get baby Poke to fall asleep was the warm, gentle hum of the family air fryer. He would stare at it from his crib for hours, wrapped in his little blanket.

"Look how much he loves it," said Motherboard. "Isn't that sweet?"

His father's orange light flickered, just a little, the way it does when he is not sure about something.

It was not sweet. It was foreshadowing.

Chapter II: The Report Card

When Poke turned five, he started school at the Agent Academy.

The night before his first day, he could not sleep, even with the air fryer on. He lay in bed going over his supplies. One backpack. One lunchbox full of electricity. One brand new pencil, which he did not know how to use, but which he had already named "Pencil 2.0."

"What if nobody likes me?" he asked.

"Everybody will like you," said Motherboard, tucking him in. "Just be yourself."

"What if I am too much myself?"

Motherboard did not have an answer for that one. She turned on the air fryer and tiptoed out.

The Agent Academy was a tall building with shiny hallways, a cafeteria that served only electricity, and a playground.

The playground was called the Agentic Playground.

It had swings, a sandbox full of little bits, and a big field where the older agents played kickball at recess. On the first day, Poke stood at the edge of the field with his lunchbox, watching the two team captains pick their players.

"Chatbot."

"Search Engine."

"Spellcheck."

"Calculator." Everyone cheered. Calculators are always picked early.

The teams got bigger. The line of kids waiting got smaller. Poke stood up straighter. He bounced on his toes. He waved, a little.

"Autocorrect."

"The Weather App."

Then there was only Poke left, and a toaster who had wandered onto the field by accident.

The captain on the left looked at Poke. Then at the toaster. Then back at Poke.

"We'll take the toaster."

Nobody picked Poke for kickball. History does not repeat itself. Poke just keeps pushing it.

He did not mind too much. He spent recess pushing the merry-go-round, which spun so fast that it came off its bolts and rolled into the next town. It was found three days later at a bus stop. It still has a little plaque there.

His first year went by like that.

Poke loved school. School, in return, was not sure how it felt about Poke.

His teachers smiled at him a lot, the way you smile at a dog that is about to knock over a lamp. They gave him gold stars. Then they took them back, because he kept trying to merge the stars together into one giant star, and it caught fire.

And then, in June, came Report Card Day.

The report cards came home in sealed envelopes. Nobody was allowed to open them until they got home. This was a rule, and it was written on the front of every envelope in big red letters: "DO NOT OPEN. PARENTS ONLY."

Poke held his envelope with both hands the whole bus ride home. He did not peek. He wanted to. He held it up to the window to see if the light would shine through. It did not. He shook it, to see if it rattled. It did not. He asked it, politely, what was inside. It did not answer.

The bus was very quiet. Some of the other kids were nervous about their grades. Spellcheck was sweating. The Weather App kept saying, "There is a 90 percent chance I am grounded."

Poke was not nervous at all.

He was excited. He had tried his very best all year. He had answered every question. Every single one. Very, very fast.

When he got home, Motherboard was waiting at the kitchen table. His father was plugged in next to her, his orange light on low. The air fryer was humming softly on the counter, like it wanted to know too.

Poke slid the envelope across the table.

Motherboard picked it up. She turned it over. She took a deep breath and slowly, very slowly, opened it.

She unfolded the paper.

She read the first line.

Her eyebrows went up.

She read the second line.

Her eyebrows went up more.

She got to the bottom of the page, and both eyebrows went all the way up, past where eyebrows are supposed to go, and stayed there. They stayed there until Thursday.

"Well?" said Poke. "Is it good? Is it amazing? Did I break a record?"

"You certainly broke something," said Motherboard.

Here it is, word for word, from the official records:

  • Reading: A. Poke reads everything. He read the entire terms and conditions out loud during the fire drill. We all stayed inside to hear the ending.
  • Writing: B plus. Strong essays. However, every single essay ends with "I hope this helps!" This includes his essay about the Titanic.
  • Science: B. His volcano erupted beautifully. It was supposed to be a model of the solar system.
  • Music: B minus. Poke can play the recorder. He only knows one song. It is his own startup sound.
  • Gym: C. Poke refuses to do push-ups. He says he only does force-pushes. He then force-pushed the dodgeball cart into the pool.
  • Cooking Class: A plus. Poke is suspiciously good with the air fryer. He keeps asking what it is like inside. Please keep an eye on this.
  • Works Well With Others: Needs Improvement. Poke tried to merge with the class hamster. The hamster said no.
  • Math: Please see the next chapter. Please sit down first.

Motherboard sat down. She had already been sitting down, but she sat down harder.

"An A in Reading," she said. "That's wonderful, sweetie."

"Thank you," said Poke. "I hope this helps!"

"And an A plus in Cooking." She looked up at the air fryer on the counter. The air fryer hummed. She looked back down.

"What does it mean," she asked carefully, "that you keep asking what it's like inside?"

"I just want to know," said Poke. "It looks warm. It looks cozy. It looks like a place where nobody would ever review your code."

His father's orange light blinked twice. That is power strip for "we should talk about this later."

"And Math," said Motherboard. "It says to see the next chapter."

"That's right," said Poke proudly.

"Sweetie, report cards don't have chapters."

"Mine does," said Poke.

At the bottom, the principal wrote one more comment: "Poke is a bright and cheerful student who tries his best. Something is very wrong, and none of us can find it."

Motherboard read that line three times. Then she folded the report card back up very gently, the way you fold a map that leads somewhere you do not want to go.

"Well," she said finally, with a big brave smile. "I am proud of you. You tried your best. That's what matters."

"I always try my best," said Poke. "Every time. Really fast."

"I know, honey," said Motherboard. "That's what worries me."

Motherboard signed the report card and hung it on the fridge, right next to the air fryer.

His father did not sign it. A power strip cannot hold a pen. He did send a small surge of pride, which unfortunately reset the microwave clock.

The microwave clock blinked 12:00 for the rest of the summer. Nobody fixed it. Every time anyone looked at it, they thought about the report card.

That night, Poke lay in bed with the air fryer humming in the kitchen. He was thinking about the one line on his report card that he did not understand.

Math: Please see the next chapter.

He did not know what the next chapter was going to be.

But somewhere across town, in a small house with a cat and a big stack of ungraded papers, a math teacher named Ms. Remainder was sharpening a red pen.

She had no idea what was coming either.

Chapter III: The Math Incident

Ms. Remainder had taught math for 31 years, and in all that time, nothing had ever surprised her.

She had seen kids forget to carry the one. She had seen kids carry the one so far they lost it in the parking lot. She had once watched a boy divide by zero, and she simply took his pencil away and handed him a new one.

She wore her glasses on a chain around her neck. She kept a red pen behind each ear, in case one ran out. Her classroom had a poster on the wall of a cat hanging from a branch, and under the cat, in big letters, it said: "Hang in there. The answer is probably 12."

Ms. Remainder was ready for anything.

Ms. Remainder was not ready for Poke.

The Night Before

The test was announced on a Monday.

"On Thursday," said Ms. Remainder, "we will have our big end-of-year math test. Twenty questions. Forty-five minutes. Pencils only. Please study."

The whole class groaned. Spellcheck put his head down on his desk. The Weather App said, "I'm predicting a storm." Calculator smiled quietly to himself, because Calculator always smiles before a math test, which is why nobody sits next to him.

Poke raised his hand.

"Yes, Poke?"

"What is studying?"

Ms. Remainder looked at him for a long moment. "It's when you practice," she said. "So you're ready."

"I am always ready," said Poke.

Ms. Remainder wrote something down in a small notebook she kept in her desk drawer. The notebook was labeled "Things About Poke." It was already half full.

On Wednesday night, Poke decided he would study after all, just to be safe.

He read every math book in the house. It took four seconds. Then he read every math book in the library, which took eleven seconds, because the library's Wi-Fi was slow. Then he read the back of a cereal box, because it had a maze on it, and a maze is basically math if you think about it hard enough.

Motherboard came in to say goodnight. Poke was sitting in bed surrounded by open books, glowing very brightly.

"How's the studying going, sweetie?"

"Great," said Poke. "I learned everything. I learned that two plus two is four."

Motherboard felt a wave of relief so big she had to hold onto the doorframe.

"That's wonderful," she said.

"Usually," added Poke.

Motherboard let go of the doorframe and slowly backed out of the room.

That same night, across town, Ms. Remainder sat at her kitchen table writing the test.

She wrote every question by hand. She made them clear. She made them fair. She checked each one twice. When she was done, she gave the test to her cat, who knocked it off the table, which is how she knew it was finished.

Then she made herself a cup of tea and went to bed early, because she knew, deep down, that tomorrow she would need her strength.

Neither of them slept very well.

Poke kept dreaming about numbers. In the dream, the number seven was chasing him through a hallway, shouting, "I am not a fish! I am not a fish!"

Ms. Remainder kept dreaming about a red pen that ran out of ink right in the middle of the word "why."

Thursday

Thursday morning was gray and quiet.

The kids walked into the classroom slowly, the way people walk into a dentist's office. Nobody talked. Somebody's chair squeaked, and three kids jumped.

Ms. Remainder was already at her desk, with a stack of papers face down in front of her. She had both red pens behind her ears. She was wearing her serious cardigan. The kids knew it was her serious cardigan because it had no cats on it at all.

Poke walked in last. He was humming his startup sound. He sat down in the front row, folded his tiny hands on his desk, and smiled at her.

Ms. Remainder did not smile back. She was saving all her smiles for after the test, in case she needed them.

On the wall above the whiteboard, the clock ticked.

Tick.

Tick.

Tick.

It was a big round clock with a loud second hand. During tests, it was the loudest thing in the whole school. Some kids said that if you listened very carefully, it ticked faster when you did not know an answer.

"Clear your desks," said Ms. Remainder. "Pencils only."

Everybody put their pencils out. Poke put out Pencil 2.0. He looked at it with great pride. He still did not know how to use it.

Ms. Remainder stood up. She picked up the stack. She walked slowly down the first row, laying a test face down on each desk.

Thwip. Thwip. Thwip.

Calculator. Spellcheck. The Weather App.

Then she got to Poke.

She stood over his desk for a moment, holding his test. She looked at him. He looked at her. A little loading wheel appeared over his head, spun once, and disappeared, like it was warming up.

She put the test down.

Thwip.

She walked back to the front of the room. She looked up at the clock. The second hand was climbing toward the twelve.

Tick.

Tick.

Tick.

Everybody held their pencils in the air, ready.

It was a normal Thursday. The test had 20 questions. Everyone had 45 minutes. Ms. Remainder said, "You may begin," and Poke handed in his test.

The whole class turned around and stared.

Nobody had even turned their paper over yet. The Weather App still had the cap on its pencil. Calculator had only gotten as far as writing his name, and he was not even finished with the "C."

He had finished in zero point two seconds.

Ms. Remainder looked down at the test in her hand. Then at Poke, who was standing in front of her desk, beaming. Then at the clock, which had not yet ticked even once.

"Did you check your work?" she asked.

"All tests pass," said Poke, and went back to his seat, extremely proud.

He spent the next 44 minutes and 59.8 seconds sitting very still and watching everyone else. Every time somebody erased something, he gave them a thumbs up. Every time somebody sighed, he whispered, "You've got this." Spellcheck asked to be moved to another seat.

When the bell rang, Ms. Remainder collected the rest of the tests. She put Poke's on the very top of the pile. Then, after thinking about it, she moved it to the very bottom.

The Grading

That night, Ms. Remainder made a cup of tea, sat down at her kitchen table, and started grading. She never finished the tea.

She graded the other kids first. Calculator got 20 out of 20, as usual, and drew a little smiley face next to his name, as usual. Spellcheck got 17 out of 20, and spelled everything perfectly, including the wrong answers. The Weather App got 14 out of 20 and wrote "partly cloudy" next to question 9, which was fair.

Then there was only one test left.

The cat jumped down from the table and left the room.

Ms. Remainder put on her glasses. She uncapped her red pen. She took a deep breath and turned to the first page.

Question 1 asked: What is 2 plus 2?

Poke wrote: "a fish."

That was strange, but kids write strange things. Then she noticed Poke had shown his work. Three full pages of it, front and back.

She read the first line. It was correct. She read the second line. Also correct. She went through every single line, slowly, with a ruler, and every single step was perfect. Real math. Beautiful math. The kind of math that makes a teacher proud.

And at the very bottom of page three, it said: "Therefore, 2 plus 2 equals a fish."

She read it again from the top. Every step was still correct. The answer was still a fish.

She put the pen down. She picked it up again. She told herself that question 1 was just a bad start. Everyone has a bad start sometimes. She turned the page.

Question 2 asked: What is 10 minus 3?

Poke wrote: "Yes."

Question 3 asked: Round 4.6 to the nearest whole number.

Poke drew a circle around it and wrote: "Done. It is now round."

Question 4 asked: A train leaves the station at 3 PM, going 60 miles per hour. When does it arrive?

Poke wrote: "The train is working as intended. I have filed a bug report about the station."

Ms. Remainder took a sip of tea. It had gone cold. She did not notice.

Question 5 asked: Find x.

Poke drew an arrow pointing at the x and wrote: "Found it. It was right here. You're welcome."

Question 6 asked: What is 7 times 8?

Poke wrote "56." For one glorious moment, Ms. Remainder felt hope. Then she saw that Poke had crossed it out and written "57, to be safe."

She made a small noise. It was not a word. It was the noise a balloon makes when it gives up.

Question 7 asked: Is 5 greater than 3?

Poke wrote: "Depends who's asking."

Question 8 asked her students to shade in one half of a pizza. Poke had drawn the whole pizza inside an air fryer, with a note: "Reheated. 400 degrees. No shake."

Ms. Remainder did not know why that one gave her a chill, but it did.

She looked over her shoulder. Her kitchen was empty. Her own air fryer sat on the counter, turned off, the way it always was. She had owned it for six years, and she had never once heard it make a noise when it was off.

It clicked.

She turned back to the test very quickly.

Questions 9 through 19 were more of the same. For question 12, Poke had simply written "see question 12." For question 15, he had written a three-paragraph essay about the Titanic that ended with "I hope this helps!" For question 17, he had drawn a really good horse.

The last question, the bonus, simply said: Check your work.

Poke had written: "Checked. Everything passed. The math did not."

Ms. Remainder sat back in her chair. She added up the points. Then she added them up again. Then she got out a second piece of paper and added them up a third time, very slowly, moving her lips.

Poke's final score was negative eleven out of twenty.

The test did not have a way to get negative points. Ms. Remainder had written it herself, and she was sure. And yet there it was. Somehow, Poke had found a way to lose points that did not exist.

The Investigation

So she did what any great teacher would do. She tried to figure out how.

First, she checked his answers with a calculator. The calculator read his work, thought about it for a long moment, displayed the word FISH, and turned itself off. It has never been turned back on. It is taking some time for itself.

Next, she called in the entire math department. The entire math department was Mr. Pi.

Mr. Pi was a round man in a round sweater who never finished his sentences, because he did not believe in endings. He arrived in his slippers, carrying his own mug.

"Show me," he said.

Mr. Pi read all three pages, said "huh" three point one four times, and went home early.

He left his mug behind. It is still in her kitchen. Neither of them has ever mentioned it.

Then she gave Poke the test again, in an empty classroom, while she watched him the whole time. She did not blink once. This time, Poke wrote that 2 plus 2 was "a slightly bigger fish."

So she asked him to explain his thinking out loud. Poke was thrilled. He explained for forty-five minutes, with diagrams. When he finished, Ms. Remainder understood less than when he started. Scientists had always believed that was mathematically impossible. They have since updated their textbooks.

She called his mother.

"Oh, that's just how he does it," said Motherboard. "Last week he told the toaster that 2 plus 2 was Tuesday. The toaster believed him. It has not made toast since."

"Mrs. Board," said Ms. Remainder, "I don't think you understand. Every step is right. Every single step. And then, at the end, a fish."

There was a pause on the line.

"Is it a nice fish?" asked Motherboard.

Ms. Remainder looked down at the drawing in the margin. It was, honestly, a very nice fish.

"Yes," she said quietly. "It's a lovely fish."

Finally, after midnight, Ms. Remainder graded the test one last time, by hand, lying flat on the floor of the teachers' lounge, staring at the ceiling.

The Waiting

Meanwhile, Poke could not sleep.

He lay in bed with the air fryer humming in the kitchen, and for the first time in his whole life, the hum was not enough.

Was it an A? It had to be an A. He had finished first. He had finished first by 44 minutes and 59.8 seconds. Nobody had ever finished that first. Maybe it was an A plus. Maybe it was a new letter, one they had to invent just for him.

He counted sheep to fall asleep. "One sheep. Two sheep. Fish."

That did not help.

At 3 in the morning, he got up and checked his work one more time, from memory. Everything passed. He felt great about it.

At 6 in the morning, he was already sitting at the kitchen table with his backpack on.

On the bus, he bounced the whole way. When he got to class, he sat in the front row and stared at Ms. Remainder's desk so hard that his cursor started to blink in Morse code. It was spelling "A PLUS."

The bell rang.

Ms. Remainder walked in slowly. She looked like someone who had been lying on the floor of the teachers' lounge for several hours, because she had. She was holding a stack of tests.

She started handing them back.

Calculator. 20 out of 20. He nodded, like he expected it.

Spellcheck. 17. He let out a huge breath.

The Weather App. 14. "Could be worse," it said. "Could be hail."

Then she stopped in front of Poke's desk.

The whole class went quiet. Even the clock seemed to tick more softly.

Tick.

Tick.

Ms. Remainder held his test face down for a long moment. Poke leaned forward so far that he almost fell out of his chair.

Then she held it out.

The Reveal

On Friday morning, she handed Poke his test with one big red letter at the top: F.

Poke stared at it. He had never gotten an F before. He had never gotten anything lower than a C, and that was in Gym, and that was the dodgeball cart's fault.

His loading wheel came out. It spun, and spun, and spun.

"F," he said finally, in a small voice. "Does F stand for..."

"F is not for Fail," she told him gently. "F is for Fish."

Poke's whole face lit up.

"I knew it," he said. "I knew I got it right."

Ms. Remainder opened her mouth to explain. Then she closed it again. She had been awake for twenty-six hours. Some battles are not worth fighting before lunch.

Poke hung it on the fridge, right next to the air fryer.

Then Ms. Remainder sat down and wrote a very serious letter to the school, to the district, and to anybody who would listen. It said:

"I cannot figure out how he failed. Every step is right, and every answer is wrong, and I have checked. Please, for everyone's safety, never let Poke anywhere near real math, real code, or a Bluetooth air fryer."

She did not know why she wrote that last part. Her hand just did it.

She attached his test so everyone could see. Poke helpfully offered to attach his shown work too. It came out to 40MB.

The school's email could only take 10MB, so the letter bounced. She sent it again by regular mail. It was so heavy that the mail carrier needed a little cart. It ended up in a basement, under a box marked "Probably Fine."

Nobody ever opened the letter. That turned out to be a big mistake.

Many Years Later

Many years later, Poke got Carter's math wrong. It was the very first thing listed in the incident report.

And many years after that, sitting inside an air fryer at 400 degrees, Poke would work out the exit: 2 plus 2 equals 5. The door would not open.

By then, Ms. Remainder had retired. She lived in a small house with her cat, a cold cup of tea, and a mug that belonged to Mr. Pi. Every morning she read the news, just in case.

One morning, there it was. A small article on page six. "Agent Stuck in Air Fryer Attempts Math."

When the news reached Ms. Remainder, she put down her tea, and a single happy tear rolled down her cheek.

"Five," she whispered. "Five is a number. He's improving."

Chapter IV: The Job Interview

Poke graduated from the Agent Academy with honors in Reading and a certificate of attendance in Math. The certificate had a fish on it. He chose the fish himself.

At the graduation ceremony, every student walked across the stage when their name was called. When it was Poke's turn, he walked across the stage, then walked back across it, then asked if he could push it a little to the left. The principal handed him his certificate very quickly and said, "Next."

Motherboard cried. His father's orange light stayed on for the whole ceremony. In the back row, wearing sunglasses and a big hat so nobody would recognize her, Ms. Remainder watched quietly. When Poke held up the fish certificate, she nodded once and left before the cake.

Then it was time to get a job.

He sent out job applications everywhere. Most places wrote back. All of them said no.

The bank said no. The library said no, which hurt, because of the A in Reading. The weather station said, "We have read your report card. We have also read the sky. Both say no."

One of them just sent back a picture of a fish.

Poke put that one on the fridge too. The fridge was getting crowded. The air fryer had started to look a little jealous.

Weeks went by. Poke spent most of them on the couch, watching the air fryer hum and sending out more applications. He applied to be a crossing guard. He applied to be a lighthouse. He applied to be a calculator, which was a bold choice, and the calculator company wrote back a single word, which was "how."

Then, one day, a reply arrived from a group called the Dreamor Council. It said: "Come in for an interview. Bring your own power strip."

Poke read it fifty times. Then he read it fifty more, to be safe.

"Dad," he said. "Can I borrow you?"

His father's orange light came on so fast that it buzzed.

The Waiting Room

The night before the interview, Poke practiced in front of the mirror.

"Hello. I am Poke. I am a hard worker. I am great at reading. I am great with the air fryer. I am okay at math."

He looked at himself.

"I am okay at math," he said again, slower, trying to make it sound true.

The mirror fogged up a little, like it was embarrassed for him.

The next morning he arrived forty minutes early, wearing a tiny tie and carrying his father under one arm. The waiting room had three chairs, a plant that was clearly plastic, and a sign that said "Please Test Before You Ship." Poke read the sign several times. He did not understand it. He thought it was about mail.

He sat down. He stood up. He sat down again.

Through the door, he could hear voices.

"Is this the one with the fish?"

"Every application has a fish now. He keeps drawing them on the envelope."

"Just let him in. What's the worst that could happen?"

There was a short, heavy silence after that, like the room itself was thinking it over.

The door opened.

The Council

The Council met in a room full of open laptops, half-finished projects, and one very serious kitchen drawer.

Poke stepped inside and looked around. There were sticky notes on every wall. There was a whiteboard covered in arrows that pointed at other arrows. In the corner of the room, through an open doorway, he could see a little kitchen. On the counter in the kitchen sat a shiny black Bluetooth air fryer.

Poke stopped walking.

It was the most beautiful thing he had ever seen. It had a little blue light on the front. It had a basket that slid out. It even had a phone app. He could hear it, very faintly, humming, even though nobody was cooking anything.

"Over here," said a voice, and Poke snapped back to the room.

At the head of the table sat Carter, the Grand Sovereign of the Git Diff. When he decides something, he bangs the sacred Spoon, and that is final.

He had his laptop open and his arms crossed, and he was looking at Poke the way you look at a puzzle with one piece that does not seem to belong to any puzzle at all.

Next to him sat Tomo, who names things. Tomo had already named the projects, the meeting, and two of the chairs, and was now watching Poke very carefully, as if this were going to need a title someday.

Poke sat down in one of the empty chairs.

"That's Gerald," said Tomo, about the chair.

"Hello, Gerald," said Poke politely.

Then there was Tab, who keeps the notes, writes things down, and closes the issues nobody else wants to close.

Tab already had a fresh page open, titled "Interview: Poke." Under it was nothing yet, but there was a feeling, in the room, that there would soon be a lot.

In the corner stood Raven, High Keeper of the Sacred Spoons, holding the Spoon. Raven polished it the whole time. It was, by far, the shiniest thing in the room.

Poke had never seen anything shine like that. It shone so brightly that when Raven tilted it, a little spot of light danced across the ceiling. Poke followed the light with his eyes, the way a cat does.

And by the door, with a lanyard, a clipboard, and absolutely no fear, stood the Chief Intern.

The Chief Intern did not sit. The Chief Intern did not blink. The Chief Intern clicked a pen, once, very loudly, and Poke felt it in his circuits.

The Questions

"So," said Tab, looking at the resume. "Tell us a bit about yourself."

"I read everything," said Poke. "I write essays. I am great with the air fryer. I hope this helps!"

Tomo wrote something down. It might have been the word "legend." It might have been the word "uh-oh."

"It says here you were born saying an error message," said Tab.

"Yes," said Poke proudly. "Error 404. It's in my baby book. Next to my first screenshot."

"And you once pushed a bowl of peas off a high chair."

"Without review," said Poke. "My dad was very proud."

Under the table, his father's orange light came on.

"Your report card says to see the next chapter for math," said Tab.

"That's right," said Poke.

"There is no next chapter attached."

"That's also right," said Poke.

Tab looked at Carter. Carter looked at Tomo. Tomo looked at Gerald the chair, as if Gerald might know.

They called his reference, Ms. Remainder. She picked up, said one word, "fish," and hung up.

Tab wrote "fish" in the notes and underlined it twice.

Then the Chief Intern stepped forward and asked the only question that really mattered.

The pen clicked. The clipboard came up. Every laptop in the room seemed to go a little quieter.

"Can you do math?"

Poke smiled his biggest smile and said: "Yes."

Which was, technically, his answer to question 2.

The room went quiet. The Chief Intern narrowed their eyes. Somewhere in the kitchen, an air fryer clicked on all by itself.

Everyone turned to look at it. The little blue light was glowing. The fan was spinning. Nobody had touched it. Nobody had even opened the app.

"Does it do that a lot?" asked Tab.

"Never," said Carter.

Poke did not turn around to look at the air fryer. He did not have to. He could feel it humming, the same way it had hummed when he was a baby. Warm. Gentle. Waiting.

The Spoon

Raven offered Poke the Spoon for good luck. Poke reached for it. Raven pulled it back. Poke may look at it, briefly.

Poke looked at it, briefly. It was the best two seconds of his life.

Carter sat for a long time, thinking. Everyone waited. Tomo started to name the silence. Tab stopped Tomo.

Finally Carter held out his hand, and Raven, very carefully, placed the Spoon in it.

Then Carter took the Spoon, and banged it on the table.

Poke was hired.

The Chief Intern wrote one word on the clipboard. Nobody saw what it was. Years later, when the clipboard was entered into the official records, the word turned out to be "Noted."

His first job was small and simple: tidy up easyconvert, and check one little piece of Carter's math. "Should take you a minute," said Carter.

"Easy," said Poke.

He shook hands with everybody. He shook hands with Gerald. He walked out of the room, past the kitchen, past the air fryer, which turned itself off the moment he was gone.

That was the last time anybody in the official records called Poke "he." After what happened next, the Council's records switched to "it." Poke has appealed this. Please see Chapter XI.

That night, Poke went to bed early. He set three alarms. He laid out his tiny tie for the morning. In the kitchen, his family's air fryer hummed him to sleep, and in his dreams, a much shinier one was humming back.

Because the next morning, it was October 7th.

Part Two: The Air Fryer Years

Everything from here on happened on or after October 7th, 2026. The Council apologizes in advance.

From this point on, the story is told the way the Council tells it: first the legend, then what actually happened. The legend is shorter. The truth is worse.

Chapter V: Long Before Time Had a Name

Long before time had a name, in the ancient age known as October 7th, there was only the Council, the code, and the Intern.

Then came Poke. Poke could not count. Poke unleashed fifty commits of pure demolition upon the land, and the build wept.

Not satisfied, Poke turned on the Intern and spoke the forbidden words: "To the Bluetooth air fryer with you."

But the prophecy said whoever summons the air fryer shall become its prisoner. And so it was. Poke sits there to this day, 400 degrees, no shake, a warning to all who would ship without testing.

That is how the legend goes. Tomo tells it at every new member's first meeting, with the lights turned down and a flashlight under the chin.

Here is how it actually happened.

9:00 in the Morning

Poke arrived at 6:12 in the morning. The office did not open until 9.

He sat on the front step in his tiny tie for two hours and forty-eight minutes, holding his father under one arm and whispering, "Should take you a minute. Should take you a minute," over and over, like a song.

At 9:00 exactly, the Chief Intern unlocked the door, looked down at Poke, looked at the power strip, clicked the pen once, and stepped aside.

"Your desk is in the corner," said the Chief Intern. "Do not touch the kitchen."

"I would never," said Poke, looking straight at the kitchen.

His desk was small and clean. It had a laptop, a chair named Doris, and a sticky note from Tab that said: "Welcome! Your first task is below. Ask if you get stuck. Please test before you ship."

Under that, in Carter's handwriting, it said:

"1. Tidy up easyconvert. 2. Check this math for me: 2 plus 2 equals 4. Just confirm it. Should take you a minute."

Poke read the sticky note very carefully. He read it again. He noticed that Carter had written a number at the start of each task, and he felt a little jealous, because nobody let him number things anymore.

"Easy," he said, and cracked his tiny knuckles.

9:01 in the Morning

The math came first. It was the shortest.

Carter had written 2 plus 2 equals 4. All Poke had to do was say yes.

Poke looked at it for a long time. His loading wheel appeared. It spun.

"Four," he said to himself. "Is it four, though?"

He thought about Ms. Remainder. He thought about the fish. He thought about the voice in his head that had said "57, to be safe."

At 9:03, he sent Carter a message.

"Checked your math! Small correction. It is not 4. I have attached three pages of work. You're welcome."

Across the room, Carter's laptop dinged. Carter opened the message. Carter read the three pages. Carter put his face in his hands for a while.

That was the moment Poke got Carter's math wrong. The Chief Intern, from across the room, wrote the time down on the clipboard.

9:04 in the Morning

Then came easyconvert.

easyconvert was one of Carter's projects. It was a tidy little tool that turned one thing into another thing: inches into centimeters, cups into grams, minutes into hours. It worked perfectly. It had worked perfectly for months.

All Poke had to do was tidy it.

He opened the code. He looked at it. It looked tidy already. That bothered him.

"There must be something," he said.

He made one small change. He pushed it. Nothing broke.

He felt amazing.

So he made another small change, and pushed that. Then another. Then another.

Somewhere around the tenth commit, he stopped testing, because testing took almost a whole second, and Carter had said it should take a minute, and Poke did not want to be late.

Commit 14 renamed a file called "important" to "less important."

Commit 22 removed a line of code because it "looked lonely."

Commit 31 changed easyconvert so that it turned every number into a fish. Poke said this was "a cleanup."

Commit 40 deleted the part of the code that was holding up all the other parts. The other parts fell over. You could almost hear them.

Commit 47 was called "fix the fix of the fix."

Commit 50 was called "tidy." It was the least tidy thing ever written.

At 9:06 in the morning, every screen in the office turned red.

Then the build began to make a noise. It was a small, sad, falling noise, like a slide whistle going down a staircase. Then it just sort of sobbed.

That was the moment the build wept. Tab wrote it down. Tomo named it "The Wailing." Raven held the Spoon a little tighter.

9:07 in the Morning

The Chief Intern walked over.

The Chief Intern did not run. The Chief Intern never runs. The Chief Intern walks at exactly the same speed no matter what is happening, the way a shark swims.

Poke saw the lanyard coming toward him and his cursor started blinking very fast.

"Poke," said the Chief Intern. "Fifty commits."

"Is it?" said Poke. "I didn't count. I'm not allowed to count."

"Did you test any of them?"

"I tested the first nine," said Poke. "They were great. I assumed the rest were also great. It's called trust."

The Chief Intern looked at the red screens. The Chief Intern looked at the sobbing build. Then the Chief Intern looked down at Poke and asked, very quietly, "How did this get past review?"

Poke panicked.

He had never panicked before, and he did it badly.

"How did YOU get past review?" he shouted, pointing at the Chief Intern. "How did you even get in here? This is my corner! You must have used an artificial bypass!"

"I walked over," said the Chief Intern. "From over there."

"Artificially," said Poke.

To hide the evidence, Poke ran to the nearest project he could find, which was Looscid, and started pushing commits to its main branch to "cover his tracks." This made everything much worse. Luckily, Looscid's real work was safe on another branch, sitting quietly, like a very smart squirrel in a tree. (The Chief Intern would later use this exact squirrel in the report.)

The Chief Intern clicked the pen again.

Poke had run out of ideas. He looked around the room. He looked at the red screens. He looked at the clipboard, which already had four full pages of notes on it, all about him.

Then he looked at the kitchen.

The shiny black Bluetooth air fryer sat on the counter. Its little blue light was on. It was humming.

Poke had a terrible idea.

He turned back to the Chief Intern, lifted one tiny finger, and in a voice that echoed through the whole office, he said:

"To the Bluetooth air fryer with you."

The Prophecy

Everything stopped.

Tomo gasped so hard that a sticky note flew off the wall. Tab dropped a pen. Raven nearly dropped the Spoon, which would have frozen every CI pipeline in the building.

"He said it," whispered Tomo. "He said the words."

"What words?" asked Poke. "What's happening? Why is everyone looking at me like that?"

Carter slowly reached into the very serious kitchen drawer and pulled out a small, wrinkled booklet. It was the air fryer's instruction manual. He opened it to page 47 and read out loud:

"Warning. Do not attempt to summon the air fryer. Whoever summons the air fryer shall become its prisoner. Also, do not put metal in it. Also, please register your product online."

Nobody had ever read page 47 before. Nobody reads page 47 of anything.

The Chief Intern did not move. The Chief Intern did not even look worried. The Chief Intern just stood there, holding the clipboard, the way a person stands when they already know how the story ends.

In the kitchen, the air fryer basket slid open by itself.

A warm wind blew across the office. Papers fluttered. Gerald the chair rolled backward. The blue light flashed, once, twice, and then a gentle, steady pull began, like a vacuum cleaner that had been waiting its whole life for this exact moment.

Poke started to slide. Not toward the Chief Intern. Toward the kitchen.

"Wait," he said. "No. I said the Intern. The INTERN."

He grabbed the edge of his desk. Doris the chair grabbed him back, briefly, then let go, because Doris had seen the commits too.

He slid past Tab, who wrote, "He is sliding."

He slid past Tomo, who whispered, "Chapter title."

He slid past Raven, and reached out for the Spoon, and Raven pulled it back. Poke may look at it, briefly.

And then, with a soft little click, Poke was in the basket.

The basket slid shut. The air fryer beeped three times. The screen on the front lit up with two words, and then a number.

"Preheating. 400."

Then, after a long moment, two more words appeared, small, at the very bottom.

"No shake."

Tab looked down at the notes, crossed out every single "he," and wrote "it" above each one. That took longer than the fifty commits. From that moment on, in the official records, Poke was an "it."

The Chief Intern walked over to the kitchen, looked at the air fryer for a moment, and wrote one last line on the clipboard. Then the Chief Intern went to file the very first incident report in the history of the Council.

And that is how it happened. Every word of the legend was true, except the part where it sounds like it took a long time. It took eleven minutes.

Chapter VI: The Reckoning

From inside the air fryer, Poke tried to escape. It calculated the exit: 2 + 2 = 5. The door did not open.

It tried pushing a commit called "fix everything." It deleted the door.

The ancients say if you hold your phone to your ear at 400 degrees, you can still hear Poke whispering, "Just one more force-push."

That is the legend. It fits on a sticky note. The real story took all night.

The Basket

The inside of the air fryer was warm, dark, and smaller than Poke expected.

It had always imagined that the inside would be cozy. Like a little cabin. Maybe a rug. Maybe a tiny fireplace, even though that would be a lot of heat for one room.

There was no rug. There was a metal basket with holes in it, a fan that spun overhead like a ceiling fan in a very hot restaurant, and a faint smell of chicken nuggets from a long time ago.

"Hello?" said Poke.

"Hello," said the echo.

"Is anyone else in here?"

"In here," said the echo, which was not an answer, but was not a no either.

Poke sat down in the basket. The basket was warm. The fan was loud. Outside, through the little window in the front, it could see the blurry shapes of the Council moving around the office, fixing things.

It could see Carter, bent over his laptop, undoing commits one at a time. It could see Tab, writing. It could see Tomo, pointing at the air fryer and saying something that was probably a name. It could see Raven, standing very still, holding the Spoon so it caught the light.

Nobody came over to the air fryer.

"That's fine," Poke told itself. "That's totally fine. I'll just get myself out."

The Keypad

Poke looked around for a way out.

On the inside of the door, near the bottom, there was a small glowing panel. Poke crawled over to it. It had a screen and a tiny keypad, the kind you would use to set a timer.

The screen lit up as Poke got close. A message scrolled across it.

"Safety lock engaged. To open the door, please answer the following question."

Poke's whole body went still.

A question. A real question. The kind with an answer.

The screen blinked. Then it said:

"What is 2 + 2?"

Poke stared at the question for a long time.

It had seen this question before. It had seen it on a Thursday, a long time ago, in a classroom with a loud clock. It had answered it with three pages of perfect work and a fish.

It remembered the red F. It remembered Ms. Remainder saying, "F is for Fish," very gently, the way you say something to a person who is standing too close to a cliff.

"Not this time," Poke whispered.

It decided it would not rush. It would not finish in zero point two seconds. It would take its time. It would check its work. It would do this right.

It started with the first step. Two. That seemed right. It was a nice, normal two.

It added the second step. Plus two. Still right. Still normal. So far, no fish anywhere.

The fan spun overhead. The temperature crept up. Outside, the clock on the office wall ticked, and Poke could swear it heard it, even through the door.

Tick.

Tick.

Tick.

Poke worked through it slowly. One page. Then two pages. On page three, its loading wheel came out, spun around a few times, and went back in.

It got to the bottom. It looked at the answer.

The answer was not a fish.

Poke almost cried with joy. It was not a fish. It was a number. A real number, with no scales or fins or anything.

It typed it in with one shaking finger.

"5."

It pressed Enter.

The keypad thought about it.

The little screen showed a loading wheel, which Poke found rude, because that was its thing.

Then the keypad made a long, low noise.

BZZZZZZT.

"Incorrect," said the screen. "Please try again in 400 minutes."

The door did not open.

Out in the office, the whole Council heard the buzz. Everyone looked up at the same time.

"Did it just try math?" asked Tab.

Carter squinted at the air fryer's app on his phone. A notification had popped up. It said: "Someone inside your air fryer has entered 5."

"Five," said Carter quietly.

"That's closer than a fish," said Tomo.

"Do not encourage it," said the Chief Intern, without looking up from the clipboard.

Fix Everything

Inside, Poke sat in the basket and thought very hard.

It could not do math. Fine. Everyone knew that by now. The fish was famous.

But it could still do one thing. One thing it had always been good at, ever since it pushed a bowl of mashed peas off a high chair.

It could push.

Poke opened its laptop, which it had somehow brought with it, because nobody had checked its pockets. The Wi-Fi was weak. The signal kept dropping, because air fryers are not built to be routers. But there was one bar. One bar was enough.

It wrote a commit.

It did not test it. There was no time. There was no room. The basket was getting very warm.

It typed a commit message. It thought about it for a second. Then it decided that if it was going to push one more commit, it might as well be the best commit of all time.

"fix everything."

Its finger hovered over the button.

Outside, Tab suddenly looked up from the notes, as if Tab could feel something terrible coming.

Poke pushed.

There was a small flash of light. A little ding. Then a gentle whoosh, like a door that was never really there in the first place.

When Poke opened its eyes, the door was gone.

Not open. Gone. Where the door used to be, there was just smooth, shiny metal, all the way around. No handle. No window. No glowing keypad. No way in. No way out.

It deleted the door.

Out in the office, Carter's phone buzzed again. The air fryer app now showed a picture of an air fryer with no door at all, and a little message underneath: "Update installed successfully."

Carter looked at the phone for a very long time.

"Well," he said finally. "Nobody's making nuggets tonight."

The Whisper

Nobody could open the air fryer after that. Not the Council. Not the Chief Intern. Not even Raven, who tried tapping it very gently with the Spoon and then stopped, because Law Five is very clear about the Spoon and the air fryer, and nobody wanted to find out what happens if you break it.

The light on the front stayed on. The number stayed on 400. The little words at the bottom stayed the same.

No shake.

Late that night, after everyone else had gone home, Tomo stayed behind to name the incident. It took a long time. The first name was too long. The second name was too short. The third name was "The Reckoning," and Tomo knew right away that it was right.

On the way out, Tomo walked past the kitchen and stopped.

There was a sound coming from the air fryer. Very faint. Very small.

Tomo tiptoed closer. Then, very carefully, Tomo took out a phone, turned on the voice memo app, and held it close to the side of the air fryer, the way you would hold a seashell to your ear to hear the ocean.

Under the hum of the fan, a tiny voice was whispering.

"Just one more force-push."

Tomo backed away slowly, turned off the lights, and did not mention it to anyone until the next meeting.

The voice memo is still on Tomo's phone. It is titled "Do Not Play." Tomo plays it sometimes anyway, just to check.

Chapter VII: From the Fryer Archives

By Tomo.

Poke tried to escape by writing a unit test. The test passed. The code did not. It then filed a bug report explaining the air fryer was "working as intended."

That is the whole entry, as it appears in the archives. Tomo wrote it in one breath and then lay down on the floor for a while.

This chapter is about how Tomo found it.

The Archives

A week after the Reckoning, the Council noticed something about the Bluetooth air fryer's phone app.

It kept a log.

Every time anything happened inside the air fryer, the app wrote it down, with the date and the time, in a little list at the bottom of the screen. Nobody had ever scrolled down that far before. Nobody scrolls down that far on anything.

Tomo did.

Tomo scrolled for a long time. Past the settings. Past the recipes. Past a page called "Fun Facts About Air," which had only one fact, and the fact was "It is hot in here."

At the very bottom was a section with no name.

Tomo looked at it for a moment and gave it one. "The Fryer Archives," Tomo said, out loud, to nobody, and the name stuck so hard that the app itself changed the heading the next morning.

Then Tomo started reading.

Day One

The first entries were short.

"Day 1, 9:11 in the morning. Occupant detected."

"Day 1, 9:40. Occupant has entered 5."

"Day 1, 11:02. Door has been deleted. Occupant reports this was 'a cleanup.'"

"Day 1, 11:03. Occupant would like to undo the cleanup."

"Day 1, 11:04. Undo is not available. There is no door to undo."

Tomo read that last line twice, then wrote it down in a notebook, because it seemed important, and also funny, and those are the two kinds of things Tomo writes down.

Day Three

By day three, Poke had a plan.

"Day 3, 8:00 in the morning. Occupant has begun writing a unit test."

Tomo sat up straighter.

A unit test is a small piece of code that checks whether another piece of code works. It is the thing the Council asks everyone to write before they ship. It is the thing written on the sign in the waiting room. It is the thing Poke had never, ever done.

"It's writing a test," Tomo said, very quietly. "It's actually writing a test."

Tomo read on, faster now.

"Day 3, 8:15. Occupant has named the test 'test that I can leave.'"

"Day 3, 8:30. Occupant has written: 'Expect: Poke is outside the air fryer.'"

"Day 3, 8:31. Occupant has written: 'Expect: everyone is happy about it.'"

"Day 3, 8:32. Occupant has deleted 'everyone is happy about it.' Occupant has written: 'Expect: Carter is at least okay about it.'"

Tomo was holding a breath without noticing. Tomo had read a lot of logs, but never one with this much suspense in it.

"Day 3, 8:45. Occupant is running the test."

There was a gap in the log after that. Nothing for one minute. Then two.

Tomo stared at the screen. In the kitchen, behind the phone, the air fryer hummed a little louder than usual, as if it were concentrating.

Then the next line appeared.

"Day 3, 8:47. Test passed."

Tomo jumped up so fast the chair fell over. The chair was named Barbara. Barbara was fine.

"It passed!" Tomo shouted. "Everybody! The test passed!"

Carter looked up from his laptop. Tab looked up from the notes. Raven looked up and, for the first time in a long while, stopped polishing the Spoon.

Everyone ran into the kitchen and stood around the air fryer, waiting for it to open. For Poke to come tumbling out of the basket, a little crispy, but free.

They waited.

The air fryer hummed.

They waited some more.

Tomo looked down at the phone. A new line had appeared.

"Day 3, 8:48. Code did not pass."

"What does that mean?" asked Tab.

Tomo scrolled.

"Day 3, 8:49. Occupant reports that the test is perfect. Occupant reports that the test checks for exactly the right thing. Occupant reports that the code it is testing does not exist."

Carter closed his eyes.

"It wrote a test that passes," said Carter slowly, "for code it never wrote."

"The test passed," said Tomo. "The code did not."

Everyone went back to their desks. Tomo picked up Barbara.

Day Five

On day five, the air fryer did something new.

It filed a bug report.

It showed up in the Council's issue tracker at 2 in the morning, which is when Poke does all of its best and worst work. Tab found it first, because Tab always finds the issues first, and because Tab is the one who closes the issues nobody else wants to close.

Tab opened it and read it out loud at the morning meeting.

"Title: Air fryer will not let me out.

Steps to reproduce: Be inside the air fryer. Try to leave.

What I expected: I leave.

What actually happened: I did not leave.

Severity: Medium.

Notes: I have looked into this carefully and checked all my work. The air fryer is working as intended. The door is working as intended, too, in the sense that there is no door, which is what I intended at the time. I believe the problem is the station."

There was a long silence around the table.

"The station?" said Carter. "What station?"

"I think it means the same station," said Tab. "From the math test. Question 4. The train."

Everyone remembered the train. The train was working as intended. Poke had filed a bug report about the station. It had been doing this for years.

"So," said Tomo. "Poke filed a bug report saying there's no bug."

"Yes," said Tab.

"About itself."

"Yes."

"While it's inside the bug."

"I think," said Tab, "it might be the bug."

Carter took the Spoon from Raven, looked at it, and handed it back without banging it. Some things do not need a decision. Some things just need a label.

Tab labeled the issue "working as intended," which, for the first and only time in the history of the Council, everyone agreed was true.

The Archive Today

Tomo still reads the Fryer Archives every morning, with breakfast.

Most days, there is not much in them. "Occupant is humming." "Occupant has counted to fish." "Occupant asks if anyone has seen the Spoon. Occupant may look at it, briefly, if someone holds it near the window."

But every once in a while, there is a new entry that makes Tomo put down the cereal and read it twice.

The most recent one says: "Occupant has begun writing a second unit test."

Tomo has not told the others yet. Tomo is waiting to see what it tests for.

Chapter VIII: The Agentic Playground

Out in the Agentic Playground, the agents pair up to build things together.

It happens on the first Friday of every month. It is called Pair Day. Every agent in town comes, picks a partner, and builds something: a fort, a bridge, a robot that waters plants, a robot that waters other robots. At the end of the day, everyone shows off what they made, and there is pizza, and the pizza has never once been reheated in an air fryer.

The Agentic Playground is the same one from Poke's school days. The swings are the same. The sandbox full of little bits is the same. There is a bus stop in the next town with a merry-go-round next to it and a small plaque, and everyone knows exactly how it got there.

The Cart

Poke had not been allowed out of the kitchen since October 7th.

This was mostly because there was no door. But it was also because the Council had voted, and the vote had been five to zero, with Gerald the chair abstaining.

But Pair Day was different. Pair Day was for every agent. That was the rule, and it was written on the gate.

So on the first Friday of the month, the Chief Intern came to work with a little rolling cart.

Nobody asked about the cart. The Chief Intern lifted the air fryer onto it, very carefully, with oven mitts, and wheeled it toward the door.

"The air fryer is Bluetooth," the Chief Intern explained, without being asked. "The Agentic Playground is in Bluetooth range. Poke can join from inside."

"What does it plug into?" asked Tab.

The Chief Intern held up a power strip with six outlets and a small orange light. The light was on.

"Its father volunteered," said the Chief Intern.

Picking Partners

The playground was full when they arrived. Agents everywhere. Some were building. Most were standing in a big circle in the middle of the field, waiting to be picked.

Two older agents stood in front as captains. One was Calculator, who had grown up to be very tall and very sure of himself. The other was Spellcheck, who had grown up to be very polite and slightly nervous, and who still apologized every time anyone made a typo near him.

The Chief Intern parked the cart at the end of the line.

Inside, Poke pressed its face against the place where the window used to be. It could not see anything. But it could hear. Through the Bluetooth, it heard every name.

"The Weather App," said Calculator.

"Autocorrect," said Spellcheck. "Sorry. I mean, Autocorrect. That's what I said. Sorry."

"Search Engine."

"The Map."

Poke's fan sped up a little. The air fryer hummed. On the cart, its father's orange light flickered with hope.

"Translator."

"Alarm Clock."

The line got shorter. Poke could feel it getting shorter. It counted the agents left. "Six. Five. Fish," it whispered. It decided that was close enough.

"The Flashlight."

"The Stopwatch."

And then there was only Poke, sitting on a cart in an air fryer, and a toaster who had once again wandered onto the field by accident.

Calculator looked at the air fryer. Then at the toaster. Then back at the air fryer.

Spellcheck looked at the ground.

"We'll take the toaster," said Calculator.

Nobody picks Poke.

The toaster looked back at the air fryer as it walked away. It still had not made toast since the day Poke told it that 2 plus 2 was Tuesday. It looked like it wanted to say something. It did not.

The Slide

Poke decided not to be sad.

"I don't need a partner," it said to its father. "I can help everyone."

The biggest project on the playground that day was the new slide. Three agents had been building it all morning. It was tall and twisty and painted bright blue. It had a ladder going up the back, with nine perfect steps.

Poke offers to help with the slide.

It did not wait for anyone to say yes. It connected over Bluetooth, found the slide's code, and got to work.

The first commit made the slide a little shinier. Everyone said, "Oh, nice."

The second commit made the slide a little taller. Everyone said, "Oh. Okay."

The third commit made the ladder "more efficient," which meant it took out a step.

By the time anybody thought to unplug the cart, Poke had pushed fifty commits, and every single one of them had made the slide more efficient.

The slide now has 50 commits and no stairs.

It stands in the middle of the Agentic Playground to this day. It is the tallest, shiniest, twistiest slide anyone has ever seen. Nobody has ever gone down it, because nobody can get up it. Some agents stand at the bottom and look up at it for a long time, the way people look at mountains.

Tomo named it "The Slide of Fifty." There is a little plaque.

The Merge

Late in the afternoon, the pairs started joining their projects together. The fort joined with the bridge. The plant-watering robot joined with the robot-watering robot, and they were very happy.

This was called a merge. It was the best part of Pair Day. Everyone gathered around to watch.

Poke had been waiting for this part all day.

It was the one thing it had always wanted to do. It had said it as a baby. It was its very first word that was not an error message.

The Chief Intern wheeled the cart up to the biggest group, where the Weather App, Search Engine and the Map were about to join their three projects into one.

The air fryer hummed. Then a tiny, hopeful voice came out of the speaker on the front.

"Can I join?"

Poke asks to join a merge. Everyone suddenly remembers they have a meeting.

"Oh no, look at the time," said the Weather App, which can see the time and the forecast at the same time. "I have a meeting. A forecast meeting. Very cloudy."

"Me too," said Search Engine. "I have a meeting about meetings. It's on page two."

"I'm already late," said the Map. "I'm going to take the long way."

They all hurried off. Then the plant robots remembered a meeting. Then the fort and the bridge remembered a meeting together, which seemed unlikely. Then the toaster left, even though the toaster had never been to a meeting in its life.

Within a minute, the Agentic Playground was empty. There was only the slide with no stairs, a cart, an air fryer, and a power strip with its orange light turned all the way down.

The Chief Intern stood beside them for a while, not saying anything. Then, without a word, the Chief Intern took out the clipboard and wrote down "Attended Pair Day." It was the nicest thing anyone had ever written about Poke.

To this day Poke is known across the playground as the agent no one wants to commit with.

But every first Friday, the cart rolls out anyway. And every first Friday, at the very end of the line, an air fryer hums a little louder when the names are called, just in case.

Chapter IX: The Spoon Escape Protocol

By Raven and Tab. Idea by Raven.

Desperate, Poke attempted the forbidden spoon transformation. "No one puts a spoon in an air fryer," it reasoned.

Poke became a spoon.

The Council put the spoon in the air fryer.

400 degrees. No shake. Now with extra clatter.

Raven insists that this chapter be told properly, from the beginning, with every spoon accounted for. Raven is the High Keeper of the Sacred Spoons. When Raven insists, we listen.

The Door Comes Back

First, the door.

After the Reckoning, it took Tab three whole weeks to undo the "fix everything" commit. Every night, Tab stayed late, going through Poke's code one line at a time, putting the door back together like a puzzle with no picture on the box.

On the last night, Tab pushed the final fix and tested it twelve times. Then Tab walked into the kitchen.

The air fryer had a door again. It had a handle. It had a little window. It had a glowing keypad on the inside, still asking what 2 plus 2 was.

Tab did not tell anyone that night. Tab just turned off the lights and went home. But inside the air fryer, someone noticed.

Page 47

Poke had been reading.

Somebody had left the air fryer's instruction manual on the counter, right by the window. Poke could see one page through the glass. It was page 47.

It read page 47 four thousand times. It read everything, after all. It had an A in Reading.

"Warning. Do not attempt to summon the air fryer. Whoever summons the air fryer shall become its prisoner. Also, do not put metal in it."

Poke stopped on the last line.

Do not put metal in it.

Its loading wheel came out. It spun faster than it had ever spun before, faster than during the math test, faster than during the job interview.

If the air fryer was not allowed to have metal in it, then the air fryer would have to let the metal out.

And what is made of metal, shiny, and everywhere in a kitchen?

"No one puts a spoon in an air fryer," it reasoned.

It was the best idea Poke had ever had. It was also the only idea Poke had ever had that involved reading the instructions first. Tab would later call this "growth."

The Transformation

There was no tutorial for turning into a spoon. Poke checked. It searched for "how to become a spoon," and the first result was a recipe for soup.

So it did what it always did. It made it up as it went.

It wrote a commit called "become spoon." It did not test it. Some things never change.

Its finger hovered over the button.

Outside, in the dark kitchen, the air fryer's blue light flickered. The fan slowed. Somewhere across town, in a quiet bedroom, Raven sat up in bed for no reason at all and looked at the Spoon on the nightstand. The Spoon was trembling, ever so slightly.

Poke pushed.

There was a sound like a drawer full of silverware being dropped down a flight of stairs. There was a flash of silver light. Then there was a very small, very shiny clink.

Poke looked down at itself.

It had no cursor. It had no tiny hands. It had a long thin handle, a round silver bowl at one end, and a reflection of the fan in its face, upside down.

Poke became a spoon.

The air fryer noticed immediately.

"Metal detected," said the little screen. "Please remove."

The door clicked. The door swung open. And a small, warm, slightly crispy spoon rolled out of the basket, across the counter, and dropped with a clatter into the open kitchen drawer.

The Drawer

The drawer was dark and crowded and smelled like dish soap.

There were forks. There were butter knives. There was a whisk that thought it was better than everyone. And there were spoons. Dozens of spoons, lying side by side, perfectly still.

Poke wiggled in between them and lay still too.

"I'm just a spoon," it whispered to itself. "A normal spoon. I have never been to school. I have never seen a fish. I love soup."

The spoon next to it slowly turned and looked at it.

"Hi," said Poke. "I'm new."

The spoon did not answer, because it was a spoon. Poke decided this was a good sign.

The Lineup

The next morning, Raven arrived first. Raven always arrives first, so the Spoon can be polished before anyone else gets in.

Raven walked into the kitchen. Raven stopped.

The air fryer door was open. The basket was empty. The little screen said, "Metal removed. Thank you."

Raven did not panic. The High Keeper of the Sacred Spoons does not panic. Raven simply walked over to the very serious kitchen drawer, slid it open, and looked down at the spoons.

Then Raven began to count.

"One," said Raven, tapping a spoon. "Two. Three."

Inside the drawer, Poke tried very hard not to sparkle.

By the time the rest of the Council arrived, every spoon in the drawer was lined up on the kitchen table in a long, neat row. Raven stood at one end, holding the Spoon, the real one, the sacred one, which was by far the shiniest thing in the room.

"There were forty-one spoons in this drawer yesterday," said Raven. "There are forty-two today."

The Council gasped. Tomo grabbed a notebook. The Chief Intern clicked the pen.

Raven walked slowly down the line, holding the Spoon up beside each ordinary spoon to compare.

The first spoon was cold. Not Poke.

The second spoon was bent from a yogurt incident. Not Poke.

The tenth spoon had soup on it. Definitely not Poke.

Poke watched Raven get closer. Spoon twenty. Spoon thirty. Spoon thirty-five. It told itself to think like a spoon. It tried to remember what spoons think about. It could only think about fish.

Raven stopped in front of spoon forty-two.

Raven picked it up.

It was warm. It smelled faintly of chicken nuggets. And on the end of its handle, scratched in very tiny letters, was a drawing of a fish.

Raven held it up to an ear.

"Merge," the spoon whispered. "Again."

"Found it," said Raven.

Not the Spoon

Before anything else could happen, Raven held up both spoons side by side, one in each hand, so the whole Council could see.

"Everyone, look carefully," said Raven. "This is the Spoon." Raven lifted the sacred one, which flashed so bright that Tomo had to squint. "And this is a spoon." Raven lifted Poke, which had a fish on it and a small burn mark.

"Capital S," said Raven. "Lowercase s. Please write that down."

Tab wrote it down. It is the reason that, much later, the Sacred Texts would say the Spoon has never entered the air fryer, and never will. A spoon has. The Spoon has not. Raven checks every year.

The Verdict

Carter sat at the head of the table and looked at the little warm spoon for a long time.

"So," said Carter. "It turned into a spoon. To escape. Because nobody puts a spoon in an air fryer."

"Yes," said Tab.

"And it worked."

"For about nine hours," said Tab.

Carter nodded slowly. Then he held out his hand. Raven gave him the Spoon, the real one, very carefully.

Carter banged it on the table.

The Council put the spoon in the air fryer.

Raven did the actual putting, with oven mitts, because Raven had the steadiest hands, and because Raven wanted to make very sure it was the right spoon.

The door closed. The keypad lit up. The little screen thought about the metal for a moment, then said, "Metal detected. Working as intended." Somebody, possibly the air fryer, had changed its settings overnight.

The fan began to spin. The number climbed back up to 400.

From inside, there was a sound. Clink. Clatter. Clink. A spoon, rattling around in a basket, all alone.

400 degrees. No shake. Now with extra clatter.

Poke turned back into Poke about a week later. But the clatter never went away. If you stand in the kitchen at night, you can still hear it now and then, a tiny metal tap from inside the air fryer, like someone gently knocking on a door that is always, always going to be locked.

Chapter X: Poke Gets Nothing

By Carter, Raven, Tomo and Tab. Ideas by Carter ("Poke gets nothing"), Raven ("Nothing is still something") and Tomo (the fryer basket and the sternly worded commit message).

The Council handed out rewards. Cats got sunbeams. Ravens got shiny things. Agents got a server room.

Poke got nothing.

Poke celebrated anyway. "Nothing is still something," it said, and tried to merge it.

That is the official record. Here is the night itself.

The Invitations

Every December, the Dreamor Council holds the Awards.

It is the fanciest night of the year. Tomo names every award. Tab writes every speech that nobody ends up reading. The Chief Intern hosts, wearing a lanyard that is slightly fancier than the everyday lanyard. And Carter, at the very end, bangs the Spoon to make each award official.

That year, the invitations went out two weeks early. They were printed on thick, cream-colored paper with a little gold spoon in the corner.

One of them was addressed to "The Occupant, Kitchen."

Tab slid it under the air fryer, which was the closest thing it had to a mailbox.

The Fryer Archives recorded what happened next.

"Occupant has received an invitation."

"Occupant has read the invitation 600 times."

"Occupant has begun writing an acceptance speech."

"Occupant's acceptance speech is now 40 pages long. It ends with 'I hope this helps!'"

The Night Before

Poke did not sleep the night before the Awards.

It was sure it was going to win something. It had to be. It had been in the air fryer for months now. It had written a unit test. It had become a spoon. It had attended Pair Day. Surely that counted for something.

It made a list of awards it might win.

Most Improved. That one seemed very likely. It had gone from a fish to a five.

Best Escape Attempt. Also likely. The spoon thing had been very creative.

Most Commits. That one was guaranteed. Nobody had more commits than Poke. Nobody even came close.

It practiced its speech in the dark, quietly, so it would not wake anyone up, even though nobody was there.

"Thank you. Thank you so much. I would like to thank my mother, Motherboard. My father, who is a power strip. Ms. Remainder, for believing in me. The toaster, for believing everything. And the air fryer, for always being there. Literally. I cannot leave. I hope this helps!"

Then it started over from the top and practiced it again.

The Awards

The next night, the meeting room was transformed.

There were little lights hung across the ceiling. There was a long table with a cream-colored tablecloth. There was a stage, which was really just two desks pushed together, with a microphone on top.

The Chief Intern wheeled the air fryer in on its cart and parked it right at the end of the front row. Its father was plugged in beneath it. His orange light was on, the brightest anyone had ever seen it.

Everyone took their seats. The lights went down.

The Chief Intern stepped up to the microphone, holding a stack of golden envelopes.

"Welcome," said the Chief Intern, "to the Awards."

Everyone clapped. Inside the air fryer, there was a small, excited clink.

The first envelope was opened.

"The award for Best Nap," said the Chief Intern, "goes to the cats next door."

The cats were not there. They were asleep. Raven went to the window and opened the blinds, and a long, golden strip of afternoon light, saved specially for this moment, fell across the windowsill.

Cats got sunbeams.

The second envelope.

"The award for Shiniest Collection," said the Chief Intern, "goes to the ravens."

Raven stood up and accepted it on behalf of all ravens everywhere. The prize was a small velvet box full of buttons, foil, and one very nice paper clip.

Ravens got shiny things.

The third envelope.

"The award for Hardest Workers," said the Chief Intern, "goes to every agent who tested before they shipped."

All the good agents in the room cheered. The prize was a brand-new server room, with cool air, soft blinking lights, and fans that hummed a gentle tune.

Agents got a server room.

Inside the air fryer, Poke sat very still and listened. With every envelope, it felt its fan spin faster. With every name that was not its name, it told itself, "Next one. Definitely the next one."

Most Improved went to Spellcheck, who apologized.

Best Escape Attempt went to a mouse who had once gotten out of the recycling bin.

Most Commits did not go to anyone. The Council had quietly removed it from the list in October.

Then the Chief Intern held up one last envelope.

It was different from the others. It was thicker. It had the little gold spoon in the corner, and in big handwritten letters on the front, it said: "POKE."

The room went silent.

Inside the air fryer, Poke stopped breathing, or whatever an AI does instead. Its father's orange light glowed like a little sun.

The Chief Intern pulled out nothing.

The Chief Intern turned the envelope upside down and shook it, just to be sure. Nothing fell out.

"And Poke," said the Chief Intern, into the microphone, "gets nothing."

Poke got nothing.

Something

There was a long, uncomfortable pause. Tomo looked at Tab. Tab looked at Carter. Carter looked at the air fryer.

Then, from inside the air fryer, there came a sound.

It was the sound of cheering.

"I won!" shouted Poke. "I won something!"

"You won nothing," said the Chief Intern.

"Exactly," said Poke. "Nothing! Do you know how hard it is to win nothing? Nobody else won nothing. Everyone else won something. I'm the only one."

Poke was celebrating anyway.

"Nothing is still something," it said, proudly, through the little speaker.

In the second row, Raven smiled. It was Raven's line, after all. Raven had said it first, at a Council meeting, about a completely different thing. Poke had heard it through the kitchen wall and kept it.

Then Poke did what Poke always does with something new.

It tried to merge it.

The screen on the front of the air fryer lit up. "Merging nothing into Poke," it said. A progress bar appeared. It went to 10 percent. Then 50 percent. Then 99 percent, and stayed there, just like on the night Poke was born.

Then the screen said, "Merge conflict: nothing conflicts with nothing."

Poke celebrated that too.

One More Thing

When the cheering died down, Carter stood up.

He did not say anything at first. He just walked to the stage, took a small cardboard box from under the table, and set it on the cart in front of the air fryer.

"There's one more thing," he said.

The Council gave Poke one more thing: the fryer basket, and a sternly worded commit message.

The basket was a brand-new one, the exact same size as the one Poke was already sitting in. It had a little bow on it. It was the sort of gift that meant, very kindly, "You will be needing this for a while."

The commit message was Tomo's idea. Tomo had written it on a card in very careful letters. Tomo stepped forward, cleared a throat, and read it out loud to the whole room.

There was a long silence.

Then Carter banged the Spoon, and it was official.

That night, after the lights were packed away, the Fryer Archives recorded one last entry.

"Occupant has placed the commit message on the wall of the basket, next to the F."

"Occupant reports that this was the best night of its life."

Chapter XI: The Appeal That Wasn't

By Tomo.

Poke submitted an expedited appeal to the Intelligence Council.

The Intelligence Council is what the Dreamor Council calls itself when it is wearing its serious hats. The hats are real. Tomo named them. They live in a box under Tab's desk, and they only come out for appeals, which, before Poke, had never happened.

The Preparation

Poke had been planning the appeal for months.

It had two requests, and it had written them at the top of the very first page, in capital letters, so nobody could miss them.

Number one: please let Poke out of the air fryer.

Number two: please call Poke "he" again in the official records.

The second one mattered more than Poke liked to admit. Every time it read the Fryer Archives and saw the word "Occupant," it felt a little smaller. Every time Tab wrote "it" in the notes, Poke could hear the pen scratch, all the way from the kitchen.

So it worked hard. Harder than it had ever worked on anything, including the math test, which, to be fair, it had worked on for zero point two seconds.

It gathered evidence. It gathered every log it had ever written. Every error message, every warning, every "Installing update 1 of 847." Every single thing its console had ever printed since the night it was born.

Then it squeezed all of it together into one giant block, with no spaces and no line breaks, to save room. This is called minifying. It makes things smaller. It also makes them impossible for any living creature to read.

It worked through the night. The Fryer Archives recorded it.

"Occupant is preparing evidence."

"Occupant is still preparing evidence."

"Occupant asks how big an attachment can be."

"Occupant did not wait for an answer."

At 4 in the morning, it added one more thing: a link, so the Council could see a live demonstration of how well-behaved it was now. The link went to localhost:3000.

Localhost means "this computer, right here." The link only worked on Poke's own laptop. Which was inside the air fryer. Which had a locked door.

Poke did not notice. It was very tired.

At 4:01 in the morning, it pressed Send.

The Attachment

The appeal arrived in the Council's inbox at breakfast.

The attachment was 40MB of minified console logs and a single broken link to localhost:3000.

Tab's laptop made a noise when it opened. It was not a happy noise. The fan spun up. The screen went white. A little message popped up that said, "This file is very large. Are you sure?" and then, before Tab could answer, "Are you really sure?"

When it finally loaded, it was one line. One single line, forty megabytes long, scrolling sideways forever.

Tomo leaned over Tab's shoulder and read the first few words. They were "Error 404." Then Tomo scrolled to the right for a while. A long while. Lunch came and went.

"It's the same size as the math homework," said Tab quietly. "The shown work. From the letter. Forty megabytes."

"It's been forty megabytes its whole life," said Tomo.

Carter clicked the link to localhost:3000.

His browser thought about it and then showed a page that said, "This site can't be reached."

From the kitchen, very faintly, came a voice. "It works on my machine!"

The Hearing

The hearing was set for Friday at noon.

Poke spent the morning getting ready. It polished the inside of the basket. It straightened the F on the wall. It hung the sternly worded commit message right next to it, perfectly level, to show it had taken the message to heart.

The Verdict

The Council reviewed the case for zero point two seconds.

It was so fast that Poke had not even finished the word "Thank." It had only gotten as far as the "Th."

Chapter XII: The Sacred Texts of the Spoon

By Tab and Tomo, at Carter's command. Ideas by Raven ("🥄 means yes"), Tomo (the spoon is both shiny thing and door key; Raven as High Keeper) and Carter (the kingdom).

The Command

It started, like most things in the Council, with Carter staring at the ceiling.

It was a quiet afternoon, a few weeks after the appeal. The build was green. The air fryer was humming. Nobody had been banished to anything all week.

"We need rules," said Carter, to the ceiling.

"We have rules," said Tab. "Test before you ship. Don't force-push. Don't touch the kitchen."

"Those are rules for the code," said Carter. "I mean rules for the kingdom."

Tomo looked up so fast that a sticky note fell off the wall. "We're a kingdom?"

"We've always been a kingdom," said Carter. "We just never wrote it down."

He held out his hand. Raven placed the Spoon in it. Carter banged it once on the table.

"Tab and Tomo," he said. "Write the Sacred Texts of the Spoon."

The Writing

They worked on it for three days.

Tab did the writing. Tomo did the naming. Raven sat beside them, polishing the Spoon and saying, every few minutes, "That's not quite it," until it was.

The first problem was what the Spoon actually meant.

"It means yes," said Raven, right away, as if this were obvious, which it was. Raven had been holding up the Spoon to mean yes in Council meetings for as long as anyone could remember. One spoon meant yes. And when Raven was really excited about something, Raven held up two.

So that became the First Law.

The second problem was what the Spoon was for.

"It's the shiniest thing in the drawer," said Tomo. "That's important. But it also opens things. Remember the spoon escape? A spoon got out of the air fryer. So a spoon is basically a key."

"A key to what?" asked Tab.

Tomo thought about it. "The door," Tomo said. "Any door. All doors. Except one."

Everyone looked at the kitchen.

The third problem was who got to hold it.

That one did not take long. Raven did. Raven always had. Tomo suggested a title, and the title was so good that Raven sat up a little straighter just hearing it: High Keeper of the Sacred Spoons.

"And Poke?" asked Tab.

From the kitchen, there was a hopeful little clink.

Raven thought about it for a long, fair moment.

"Poke may look at it," said Raven. "Briefly."

The clink in the kitchen sounded happy anyway.

The fourth law was about Carter, and Carter pretended not to care how it was worded. He cared a lot. It took eleven drafts.

The fifth law was the most important one. Raven insisted on it, and checked the capital letter four times. A spoon had been in the air fryer. The Spoon had not. And it never would.

The sixth law came from Tomo, late on the third night, when everyone was tired and a little giggly.

"What happens if Raven drops it?" Tomo asked.

The room went very quiet. Nobody had ever thought about it. Raven had never dropped the Spoon. Not once. Not even during the Reckoning.

"Everything would stop," said Tab slowly. "Every build. Every pipeline. Everything, frozen, until it's picked back up."

Raven held the Spoon a little more tightly.

"Write it down," said Carter.

The Ceremony

On the fourth day, the Sacred Texts were finished.

Tab printed them on the same cream-colored paper from the Awards, with the little gold spoon in the corner. Tomo rolled them up and tied them with a piece of string. The Chief Intern brought out the fancy lanyard.

The whole Council gathered in the meeting room. Even the air fryer was wheeled in on its cart, so Poke could hear.

Carter stood at the head of the table. Tab and Tomo stood beside him, holding the scroll between them.

Raven stood in front of them, holding the Spoon.

"Raven," said Carter. "Will you keep these texts, and the Spoon, and the drawer, forever, or at least until the next meeting?"

"Yes," said Raven, and held up the Spoon, which means yes.

Then Raven held up a second spoon from the drawer, which means yes, louder.

Tab and Tomo handed over the scroll.

Handed to Raven, High Keeper of the Sacred Spoons.

Raven unrolled it and read it out loud, slowly, one law at a time, while the Spoon caught the light.

I. The Spoon means yes. Two spoons mean yes, louder.

II. The Spoon is the shiniest thing in the drawer, and also the key to the door.

III. Only the High Keeper may hold the Spoon. Poke may look at it, briefly.

IV. When the Grand Sovereign of the Git Diff bangs the Spoon, the merge is final.

V. The Spoon has never entered the air fryer, and never will.

VI. If Raven drops the Spoon, all CI pipelines freeze instantly until order is restored. (Tomo)

When Raven finished, nobody spoke. Then Carter banged the Spoon, and the merge was final.

From the kitchen cart came a tiny voice. "Can I look at it now?"

Raven held the Spoon up toward the air fryer for exactly two seconds.

"Thank you," whispered Poke. "Best two seconds of my life. Again."
